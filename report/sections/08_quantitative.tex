\subsection{State tracking and length generalisation (RQ2)}
Table~\ref{tab:state} reports token accuracy at the training length (64) and at 4$\times$ and 8$\times$ that
length. Chance level is $1/|G|$: 0.5 for $\mathbb{Z}_2$, 0.333 for $\mathbb{Z}_3$ and 0.167 for $S_3$.

\begin{table}[htbp]\centering
\resizebox{\linewidth}{!}{\input{tables/state_tracking}}
\caption{RQ2: token accuracy on group word problems (mean $\pm$ std over 3 seeds). Models are trained on length
64 and tested on lengths 64, 256 and 512. Bold: within 0.005 of the best in the column. The lower block contains
the two extensions. All recurrent models have the same state size (8192 floats).}
\label{tab:state}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Negative eigenvalues fix parity.} With decays restricted to $(0,1)$ the SSM reaches only 0.867 on
parity even at the training length and falls to chance (0.546) at length 512, as predicted by the proposition
in Section~\ref{sec:math}. Allowing $a_t\in(-1,1)$ changes nothing else in the model, yet it solves parity on all
three seeds at every length (0.999 at 512, i.e.\ 8$\times$ the training length). Gated DeltaNet behaves the same
way: with $\beta\in(0,2)$ it keeps 0.987 at length 512, with $\beta\in(0,1)$ it falls to 0.586.

\paragraph{Fitting the training length is not the same as learning the rule.} Gated DeltaNet with
$\beta\in(0,1)$ reaches 0.997 on parity at length 64, nearly perfect, but only 0.586 at 512. Its transitions
cannot represent a sign flip, so it must have learned a solution that only works for short sequences. The
Transformer shows the same pattern on $\mathbb{Z}_3$ (0.991 at 64, 0.422 at 512). Judged by training-length
accuracy alone, these models would be ranked far too high.

\paragraph{DeltaProduct learns the non-abelian group.} $S_3$ separates the models most clearly. Only the
delta-rule models with $\beta\in(0,2)$ fit it at all. Gated DeltaNet does so on two of three seeds (1.000 and
0.999 at length 64) but fails on the third (0.446), and it generalises poorly (0.51, 0.83 and 0.28 at length 512,
mean 0.542). DeltaProduct with two Householder steps per token fits $S_3$ on every seed and keeps
$0.994\pm0.001$ at length 256 and $0.912\pm0.014$ at length 512. Both ingredients are necessary: DeltaProduct with
$\beta\in(0,1)$ stays near chance on $S_3$ (0.209 at 512), because a product of matrices with eigenvalues in
$(0,1]$ still cannot reflect.

\paragraph{$\mathbb{Z}_3$ resists every model.} Several models fit $\mathbb{Z}_3$ at length 64 (Transformer
0.991, Gated DeltaNet 0.996, DeltaProduct 0.998, rotational SSM 0.978), but none generalises: at length 512
all lie between 0.37 and 0.52. This includes the two models whose transitions can represent a rotation by
$120^\circ$, the rotational SSM and DeltaProduct with $\beta\in(0,2)$. We return to this in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\paragraph{The rotational SSM.} Rotations include the sign flip ($\theta=\pi$), yet the rotational SSM solves
parity on only one of three seeds (0.628 on average at 512, against 0.999 for the negative-eigenvalue SSM).
Table~\ref{tab:rotation-length} varies its training length on $\mathbb{Z}_3$: it fits short sequences perfectly
(1.000 at length 16, 0.995 at 32) but becomes unreliable at 64 (0.719, two of three seeds fail), and no training
length leads to generalisation (0.38--0.43 at length 512, chance 0.33).

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.62\linewidth]{figures/recall_capacity}
\caption{Recall accuracy against the number of key--value pairs to remember. The SSM breaks once the pairs
outgrow its state; Gated DeltaNet does not break at 64 pairs despite having half the state of the larger SSM.}
\label{fig:recall}
\end{figure}

Attention with a short convolution solves every size, within 500 steps each time: retrieving one of 64 facts is
as easy for it as one of 16, because it simply looks the key up. The SSM shows a clear capacity limit. With
$N=16$ (8192 floats of state) it solves 16 pairs but not 32 (0.134); with $N=64$ (32{,}768 floats) it solves 16 and
32 pairs but not 64 (0.053). Quadrupling the state moves the breaking point from between 16 and 32 pairs to between 32 and 64,
which is the behaviour predicted by the fading-memory picture of Section~\ref{sec:theory}. Gated DeltaNet solves all three
sizes, including 64 pairs (0.991), with 16{,}384 floats of state, half that of the SSM that fails. Its
erase-then-write update stores associations far more economically than the SSM's additive accumulation. The
steps needed to reach 90\% grow with the number of pairs for every recurrent model (DeltaNet: 1500, 2500, 6500),
so harder recall is also slower to learn.

\paragraph{Training time.} At every measured length up to 16{,}384 tokens the attention layer is the fastest:
its forward pass takes 31\,ms at 16k tokens against 56\,ms for the SSM and 276\,ms for Gated DeltaNet. The
asymptotics are nevertheless visible. From 4k tokens on, attention's forward time grows by about $3\times$ per
doubling of the length (3.2, 9.4, 31.2\,ms), entering its quadratic regime, while the SSM's grows by
$2\times$ (14, 28, 56\,ms). Extrapolating these rates, the two cross near 32k tokens, a length at which our
unfused implementation no longer fits in 6\,GB. The SSM's forward-plus-backward time grows faster than linearly
beyond 4k tokens (86, 273, 948\,ms), which we attribute to memory pressure from the large intermediate tensors of
the chunked PyTorch code: the SSM layer needs 2.7\,GB at 16k tokens against 0.5\,GB for attention.

\paragraph{Generation.} The attention KV cache grows linearly with the context, from 512\,KB at 128 tokens to
32\,MB at 8192 tokens per layer (batch 4), while the SSM and DeltaNet keep 512\,KB and 256\,KB regardless of
context. Per-token latency, however, barely changes: attention takes 1.18\,ms at 128 tokens and 1.38\,ms at 8192.
At this scale generation is dominated by the fixed overhead of launching many small GPU kernels, not by reading
the cache, so the recurrent models win on memory but not yet on speed.

\paragraph{The Triton kernel.} For long sequences the Triton kernel is $1.33$--$1.49\times$ faster than the
chunked PyTorch scan and uses $4.3$--$4.8\times$ less memory, because it never materialises the chunk-level
matrices. It reaches only about 9\,GB/s, roughly 5\% of the GPU's memory bandwidth. Section~\ref{sec:discussion}
explains why.
